# Storyworld Narrative Taxonomy

Storyworld's promise is that a user can bring any narrative to life in any medium, format, length, and style they want. This taxonomy is the classification system behind that promise. It treats a narrative work as a **composition of two things**:

1. **Narrative substance** — what kind of story it is: its family, engine, structure, emotional arc, and knowledge frame. Substance is medium-neutral: the same mystery can be a podcast, a carousel, a feature film, or a novel.
2. **Realization** — how the story meets its audience: medium, format and length class, realization style, presentation mode, release model, audience role, and continuity model.

No dimension implies another. Every family can be realized in every medium, at every length, in every style — the platform's job is to keep canon, rights, and continuity intact while a story moves across them.

The reusable creative mechanisms (story engines, narrative structures, emotional arcs, knowledge-framing patterns) are cataloged in the companion document `narrative-building-blocks.md`. The composition formula at the end of this document references both.

# Part I — Narrative substance

## 1. Core Narrative Families

### A. Knowledge and Explanation

Stories organized around understanding something.

* **Historical reconstruction** — events, civilizations, wars, biographies, forgotten episodes.
* **Causal systems explanation** — geopolitics, economics, technology, science, infrastructure, social systems.
* **Conceptual inquiry** — philosophy, ethics, consciousness, identity, paradoxes, thought experiments.
* **Theological or mythic interpretation** — scripture, symbolism, religious history, spiritual meaning.
* **Practical education** — safety, health, professions, engineering, habits, skills, and how things work.
* **Comparative explanation** — eras, ideologies, products, cultures, systems, or levels compared.

### B. Interpretation and Revelation

Stories claiming that the familiar surface hides a deeper meaning.

* **Hidden-meaning interpretation**
* **Symbolic or archetypal reading**
* **Cultural and media analysis**
* **Counterintuitive reframing**
* **Myth-busting or misconception correction**
* **Forbidden, forgotten, or suppressed knowledge**
* **“This is really about…” revelation**

Typical movement: familiar subject → contradiction → deeper pattern → transformed understanding.

### C. Investigation and Mystery

Stories driven by uncertainty and discovery.

* Detective mystery
* Historical mystery
* Scientific mystery
* Conspiracy investigation
* Clue-based puzzle
* Search-and-find adventure
* Secret-room or hidden-object discovery
* Unreliable testimony and contested truth
* Atmospheric or liminal mystery
* Light ARG or audience-assisted investigation

### D. Adventure, Quest, and Rescue

Goal-oriented stories involving obstacles and movement.

* Journey or expedition
* Treasure hunt
* Rescue mission
* Escape story
* Survival journey
* Exploration of unknown places
* Monster, villain, or disaster confrontation
* Team mission
* Return-home or restoration quest

### E. Character and Relationship Stories

Stories centered on people changing, connecting, or navigating life.

* Family slice-of-life
* Childhood discovery
* Friendship and teamwork
* Sibling rivalry
* Parent-child bonding
* Coming-of-age
* Emotional milestone
* Everyday conflict and reconciliation
* Fear, grief, jealousy, honesty, or responsibility
* Romance, reunion, celebration, birth, loss, or transition

### F. Fantasy and Imaginative Play

Ordinary reality transformed through imagination.

* Pretend-play worlds
* Toys coming alive
* Superhero adventures
* Magical transformation
* Role reversal
* Body or age transformation
* Princess, pirate, spy, monster, or fairy stories
* Portal and secret-world stories
* Real-life enactments of fictional worlds
* Speculative “what if?” scenarios

### G. Comedy and Comic Misadventure

Stories driven primarily by disruption and humorous escalation.

* Slapstick
* Absurdist comedy
* Mischief and rule-breaking
* Mistaken identity
* Escalating household chaos
* Workplace or family sitcom
* Prank and surprise
* Failed plans
* Comic rivalry
* Visual-gag or dialogue-light comedy

### H. Challenge, Competition, and Spectacle

Stories structured as games with explicit rules and stakes.

* Elimination contest
* Last-to-leave
* Endurance or survival challenge
* Race
* Team-versus-team
* Parent-versus-child
* Expert-versus-amateur
* Build competition
* Mystery-box challenge
* Physical obstacle course
* Large-prize game
* Record attempt
* Mass-participant spectacle

### I. Experiential and Lifestyle Narrative

Real experiences shaped into dramatic stories.

* Family vlog
* Travel adventure
* Day-in-the-life
* Vacation or event chronicle
* First-time experience
* Celebration or milestone
* Behind-the-scenes story
* Personal transformation
* Home, room, or lifestyle makeover
* Celebrity or collaborator experience

### J. Experiment, Creation, and Discovery

Stories built around making, testing, or encountering something.

* Science experiment
* Engineering build
* Cooking experiment
* Art or craft project
* Product or myth test
* Food tasting
* Wildlife encounter
* Collection or search challenge
* Restoration or repair
* “Can we build/do/survive this?” experiment

### K. Moral Fable and Edutainment

Entertainment organized around an explicit lesson.

* Honesty
* Sharing
* Kindness
* Safety
* Healthy habits
* Emotional regulation
* Responsibility
* Cooperation
* Courage
* Environmental stewardship
* Social skills
* Early-learning concepts

The lesson may be direct, embedded in conflict, or delivered through a closing recognition.

### L. Musical and Rhythmic Storytelling

Narrative communicated through repetition, performance, and song.

* Nursery rhyme
* Sing-along
* Dance-along
* Educational song
* Musical pretend play
* Holiday musical
* Repetition-based learning
* Song-linked episodic anthology
* Concept album and visual album

# Part II — Realization dimensions

## 3. Media and channel classes

* **Moving image** — video and film at every length; channels include YouTube, streaming platforms, social video, broadcast, theatrical, and owned sites.
* **Audio** — podcasts, audio drama, audiobooks, music, ambient sound; channels include podcast platforms, music streaming, and video platforms carrying audio-first work.
* **Written word** — from micro-posts to full books; channels include owned sites and blogs, newsletters, serialized-fiction platforms, social text, and print publishing.
* **Static and sequential visual** — single images through graphic novels: carousels, comics, picture books, photo essays, posters, cards, infographics, zines; channels include image-first social platforms, print, and galleries.
* **Interactive and playable** — swipe/tap stories, chat-message fiction, branching episodes, visual novels, conversational characters, explorable worlds, ARGs, and games proper — from micro narrative games to full titles and live-service seasons. Playable realizations cross the interactive-runtime boundary: Storyworld owns canon and authored packages; the runtime owns play state.
* **Live and time-bound** — livestreams, premieres, live readings and performances, watch parties, seasonal calendar events.
* **Physical products** — printed books, card decks, art prints, workbooks, journals, calendars, tabletop games, and narrative-bearing merchandise.
* **Hybrid and transmedia** — properties that deliberately span classes, either re-telling one story per medium (adaptation) or distributing complementary story fragments across media (transmedia).

## 4. Format and length classes

Length is a class of its own, orthogonal to family and style. Each medium has a ladder.

### Moving image

* **Micro** (≤60 seconds) — clip, gag, teaser, vertical Short/Reel.
* **Short-form** (1–5 minutes) — sketch, scene, music video, compact explainer.
* **Mid-form** (5–20 minutes) — standard web episode, webisode; festival short films extend to ~40 minutes.
* **Broadcast-length episode** (20–30 or 40–60 minutes) — sitcom-length and drama-length episodes.
* **Special / telefilm** (60–90 minutes).
* **Feature film** (80–180+ minutes).
* **Marathon and ambient long-play** (1–10+ hours) — omnibus cuts and looping ambience.

Serial forms (orthogonal to length): one-off standalone; pilot or proof-of-concept; limited/mini-series; ongoing returning series with seasons; anthology series (episodic or seasonal); vertical micro-drama serial; holiday or special episode; spin-off; crossover special; franchise/saga.

Derived units: teaser, trailer, clip, excerpt, recap ("previously on"), compilation/supercut, behind-the-scenes, bumper/interstitial. Derived units are narratives in their own right — a trailer must manage spoilers, not merely crop the master.

### Audio

* Sting or intro (under 30 seconds) → segment (1–5 minutes) → podcast episode (20–60 minutes) → audio-drama episode (15–45 minutes) → audiobook chapter → full audiobook (hours) → single → EP → full/concept album → ambient long-play loops.

### Written word

* **Fiction ladder**: micro-post (≤280 characters) → post or thread → flash fiction (<1,000 words) → short story (1,000–7,500) → novelette (7,500–17,500) → novella (17,500–40,000) → novel (40,000+) → serialized chapter fiction → multi-book series.
* **Nonfiction and editorial forms**: explainer, essay, reported feature (800–5,000 words), guide, field note, manual chapter, curriculum unit, documentary feature article, letter/epistolary dispatch, devotional or reflection, speech/toast/sermon, newsletter dispatch, companion codex or lore entry.

### Static and sequential visual

* Single image → diptych/triptych → carousel (8–10 slides) → photo essay → comic strip (3–8 panels) → comic issue or chapter (20–32 pages) → graphic novel → picture book (24–48 pages) → poster or print series → numbered card set → infographic → zine → narrative calendar (including door-per-day seasonal calendars).

### Interactive and playable

* **Interactive-story ladder**: poll or quiz beat → swipe/tap web story → chat-message fiction episode → branching episode → visual-novel chapter → conversational character session → explorable world slice → ARG season.
* **Game-form ladder**: micro or hyper-casual narrative game → demo or vertical slice → game episode or chapter → full game → expansion/DLC → live-service season.

### Live and time-bound

* Scheduled premiere; livestream episode; live reading or performance; governed in-character appearance; seasonal calendar event window.

### Physical products

* Art print; card deck or pack; picture book; chapter book; workbook or journal; door-per-day calendar; tabletop or party game; narrative packaging.

## 5. Release and seriality models

* **Run shapes**: standalone one-off; limited run; ongoing open-ended; seasonal cycles; anthology rotation; franchise/saga with spin-offs and crossover specials.
* **Cadence**: fixed schedule; batch/binge drop; rolling drops; live time-window; evergreen library versus dated topical work.
* **Re-release**: annual seasonal re-issue (versioned editions of the same property); remastered or updated editions.
* **The derivation ladder**: a pillar work spawns derived cuts down the length ladder (feature → trailer → clips; long essay → thread → post) plus promotional derivatives. Derivation resizes and repackages the *same* content, and must remain spoiler-safe against what each audience already knows.
* **Three ways a story spans formats** — kept distinct because the platform governs them differently:
  1. **Derivation** — same content resized or repackaged (renditions of one master).
  2. **Adaptation** — the same story deliberately re-realized in another medium, allowed to diverge without altering source canon (adaptation branches).
  3. **Transmedia complementary canon** — different, complementary story fragments per medium, where the full picture exists only across them (proposed pattern).

## 6. Realization styles

Style is orthogonal to family, length, and medium: any story can be realized anywhere on these spectrums. Named, reusable styles are governed by the art-style definitions feature (`../platform-capabilities/additional-features.md`).

* **Moving image spectrum**: live-action → live-action with VFX augmentation → mixed live-action and animation → puppetry, toy, and miniature staging → stop-motion → 2D animation (toon, painterly, anime-inflected) → 3D/CG animation → motion graphics and kinetic typography → photoreal generative → stylized generative → archival/found-footage → screen-capture and gameplay.
* **Static and print styles**: photography; illustration in defined styles; painterly; flat/vector; comic inking; collage; typographic.
* **Audio styles**: single-voice narration; full-cast drama; sung/musical; ambient soundscape.
* **Playable and game styles**: real-time engine-rendered 2D/3D; pre-rendered scenes; text-based or parser interactive fiction; print-and-play tabletop.
* **Written registers**: reportorial, essayistic, lyric, epistolary, archival/documentary, expository-narrative blend.

## 7. Presentation modes

The rhetorical stance of the work — distinct from narrative family and from style.

* **Moving image**: voice-over documentary; cinematic essay; host-led explainer; interview or dialogue; animated explainer; character animation; live-action skit; family vlog; reality competition; game-show format; musical performance; mixed live action and animation; toy or miniature staging; screen-based or gaming narrative; found footage, archive, diary, or field notes; short-form vertical sequence; long-form episodic film.
* **Written and static visual**: carousel, comic, illustrated sequence, or web story; picture-book spread; photo essay; essayistic article; reported feature; epistolary letter; diary or journal entry; field notes; newsletter dispatch; thread; liveblog; codex or encyclopedia entry.
* **Audio**: narrated documentary; full-cast audio drama; host monologue; interview show; sung narrative; ambient soundscape.
* **Interactive and live**: interactive or branching experience; conversational character; point-and-click adventure; narrative RPG; walking sim; puzzle-narrative; simulation or management narrative; live premiere or watch party; live reading or performance.

# Part III — Audience and continuity

## 8. Audience Roles

A Storyworld narrative may position the audience as:

* **Learner** — receiving an explanation.
* **Witness** — observing a life, event, or memory.
* **Companion** — traveling alongside the host or family.
* **Detective** — solving clues and interpreting evidence.
* **Judge** — deciding who performed best or what is true.
* **Player** — predicting, voting, choosing, or participating.
* **Confidant** — receiving secrets, reflections, or confessions.
* **Challenger** — invited to recreate the activity.
* **Moral observer** — evaluating choices and consequences.
* **Community member** — following recurring characters and shared history.
* **Collector** — assembling numbered releases and sets.
* **Correspondent** — receiving the work as addressed dispatches (letters, newsletters).
* **Contributor** — submitting material under governed permission; submissions are non-canon unless explicitly accepted.

## 9. Serialization and Continuity Patterns

* **Standalone episode:** complete story with no required context.
* **Recurring format:** same engine, new subject.
* **Recurring cast:** stable characters in separate adventures.
* **Persistent world:** events alter future episodes.
* **Seasonal arc:** multiple episodes build toward a conclusion.
* **Reveal ladder:** each installment exposes a deeper layer.
* **Quest chain:** one mission unlocks the next.
* **Anthology:** different stories joined by theme or narrator.
* **Shared universe:** separate properties intersect.
* **Multiple POVs:** the same event seen by different characters.
* **Contested truth:** incompatible accounts remain narratively meaningful.
* **Nonlinear chronology:** memories, flashbacks, loops, or reordered evidence.
* **Branching and reconvergence:** different paths eventually rejoin.
* **Interactive branch:** audience choice influences the route.
* **Adaptation branch:** one canon event becomes different medium-specific versions.
* **Deep hierarchy:** world → saga → season → arc → episode → sequence → scene → beat.
* **Living canon:** corrections, replacements, expiry, and superseded truths are preserved rather than silently overwritten.

These continuity patterns correspond directly to Storyworld's pattern fixtures, schema probes, and proposed patterns; the project-by-pattern mapping lives in `narrative-story-projects.md`.

# Part IV — Composition

## 10. Canonical Storyworld Composition

Each narrative work can be defined as:

> **Narrative Family + Story Engine + Structural Arc + Emotional Arc + Knowledge Frame + Audience Role + Medium + Format/Length Class + Realization Style + Presentation Mode + Release Model + Continuity Model**

Examples:

> **Interpretive cultural analysis** + hidden-meaning engine + revelation structure + familiarity-to-transformation arc + symbolic framing + audience as learner/detective + moving image + mid-form + cinematic essay + scheduled ongoing series + standalone recurring format.

> **Children’s fantasy edutainment** + rescue mission + moral-lesson structure + fear-to-courage arc + experiential learning + audience as companion + moving image + short-form + live-action pretend play + ongoing series + recurring cast and persistent world.

> **Comic misadventure** + escalation engine + compressed classical structure + chaos-to-relief arc + audience as witness + moving image + micro + stylized animation + short-form vertical sequence + ongoing series + recurring cast with prop-state continuity.

> **Character and relationship story** + reconciliation engine + recognition-to-hope structure + isolation-to-acceptance arc + audience as confidant + static visual + 8–10-slide carousel + illustrated sequence + recurring series + recurring cast and living canon.

> **Generational family saga** + transformation engine + classical structure + loss-to-meaning arc + audience as witness + written word + serialized chapter fiction + prose with illustrated plates + seasonal arcs + persistent world, deep hierarchy, and shared universe.

The same substance recomposed across realizations is how one property becomes a book, a film, a carousel series, and an album without losing its identity.

## 11. How Storyworld realizes the taxonomy

| Taxonomy dimension | Storyworld concept |
|---|---|
| Narrative families, engines, structures, knowledge frames | Templates, vocabularies, and validators (never free-text genre labels) |
| Realization styles | Art-style definitions and the production design and look system (`../platform-capabilities/additional-features.md`, `../platform-capabilities/production-design.md`) |
| Media, channels, format and length classes | Export adaptors and rendition specifications; presentation-unit grammars (slides, panels, pages, threads) as adaptor/template configuration |
| Release and seriality models | Arcs, publication windows, release records, and re-release editions |
| Derivation vs. adaptation vs. transmedia | Renditions; adaptation branches; complementary-canon pattern (proposed) |
| Continuity patterns | Pattern fixtures, schema probes, and proposed patterns (mapped to showcase projects in `narrative-story-projects.md`) |
| Audience roles | Participation and governance boundaries — votes, submissions, sessions, and collection records |

Genre-specific needs belong in templates, vocabularies, validators, and adapters; the taxonomy never expands the generic core schema.
